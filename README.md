# Mental Health Safety & Support Framework

A structured guide to understanding, recognising, and responding to mental distress in educational settings.

Designed as a practical framework for supporting people in distress, with a focus on reducing cognitive overload while maintaining safety, clarity, and appropriate escalation.

---

## Key Idea

People do not need perfect support - they need safe, consistent, human support.

---

## Core Principles

- Safety > Relationship > Intervention
- Behaviour is communication, not a problem
- Notice change from baseline before making assumptions
- Escalate when uncertain (safety > certainty)

## Origin

This material was developed in response to a mental health workshop delivered to UNSW Cyber Security staff.

This approach also reflects feedback from workshop facilitation, emphasising progressive learning, cognitive load awareness, and clear distinction between actionable expectations and deeper theoretical knowledge.

Following the workshop, tutors who were unable to attend requested a structured recreation of the content. This framework was developed to meet that need, drawing on clinical experience in mental health and critical care.

It aims to translate workshop-based learning and clinical insights into practical, role-appropriate strategies for educational environments, while mapping core ideas to security concepts such as threat modelling, risk prioritisation, and interpreting behavioural change as signal rather than noise.

---

## Design Approach

This framework is informed by recovery-oriented and trauma-informed principles, emphasising:

- person-centred support
- strengths-based thinking
- the importance of relationship and context

These concepts are translated into practical, role-appropriate strategies for non-clinical environments.

---

## Security Perspective

While developed in a mental health context, this framework aligns closely with security and systems thinking.

Key parallels include:

- **Threat modelling:** prioritising risks by impact rather than assumptions
- **Triage and escalation:** acting on uncertainty to maintain safety
- **Behaviour as signal:** interpreting changes as indicators, not intent
- **Non-malicious insider risk:** recognising that most risk arises from distress or cognitive load, not malicious behaviour
- **Cognitive load reduction:** structuring decisions to reduce error under stress

These parallels highlight how security principles can be applied to human behaviour in safety-critical environments.

## Evidence of Practice

This framework is informed by real-world clinical experience in high-acuity environments, including formal placement assessments demonstrating:

- strong clinical reasoning and prioritisation
- safe escalation of patient risk
- effective management of competing priorities
- recognition of subtle changes in condition

A sample of assessed clinical feedback is included in the `evidence_of_practice/` folder.

## Source Material (Slides)

The original material was developed as slide decks for workshop use with UNSW Cyber Security staff and for rapid knowledge sharing.

These slides represent early, first-draft material and prioritise completeness over clarity and usability. As a result, they are more detailed and less structured than ideal for real-world application.

The Markdown documents in this repository represent a refined version of the content, designed to:

- reduce cognitive load
- improve clarity and accessibility
- provide practical, role-appropriate guidance

For structured, simplified, and practical versions of this content, see the `docs/` folder.

Slides are included for reference and transparency of development.
