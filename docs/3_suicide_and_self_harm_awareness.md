# Suicide and Self-Harm Awareness

This document provides guidance on recognising and responding to suicide and self-harm risk in non-clinical settings.

You are not expected to:

- diagnose
- assess risk clinically
- manage the situation alone

Your role is to:

- notice
- listen
- support
- escalate when needed

---

## Core Idea

> Most people do not want to die - they want relief from distress.

Support should focus on:

- understanding the person
- reducing isolation
- connecting them to appropriate help

---

## Important Principles

- Risk is **dynamic and can change quickly**
- No tool can perfectly predict suicide risk
- Authentic engagement is more important than checklists
- Seeking support is more important than being “right”

---

## Why Someone Might Experience Suicidal Thoughts

There is no single cause.

Common contributing factors include:

- Hopelessness or lack of future
- Emotional pain or distress
- Feeling overwhelmed
- Lack of coping mechanisms
- Isolation or lack of support
- Loss (relationships, identity, stability)
- Trauma or abuse
- Difficulty expressing needs

In some cases, self-harm may be used to:

- relieve distress
- feel something instead of numbness
- communicate a need for help

---

## Risk Factors (General Awareness)

Certain factors may increase risk:

- high levels of distress
- chronic illness (physical or mental)
- isolation or lack of support
- recent loss or crisis
- unstable living or social environment
- impulsivity or difficulty regulating emotions

> Risk factors are indicators, not predictors.

---

## What You Can Do

### 1. Ask Directly

If you are concerned, ask clearly:

- “Are you thinking about suicide?”
- “Are you thinking about killing yourself?”

### Key Points

- Asking does **not increase risk**
- It shows care and concern
- It helps people feel heard

---

### 2. Listen and Stay

If the person says yes (or suggests risk):

- listen without interrupting
- allow them to express themselves
- stay with them if possible

Avoid:

- rushing to fix the problem
- giving advice too early

---

### 3. Get Help

You are not expected to manage the situation alone.

- involve colleagues or senior staff
- follow appropriate escalation pathways
- connect the person to professional support

---

### 4. Communicate Clearly

- explain what you are doing
- avoid sudden changes or disappearance
- be open about next steps

Example:

> “I’m going to get someone to help us with this.”

---

### 5. Respect Role Boundaries

Your role is to:

- support
- listen
- escalate

Your role is not to:

- assess risk clinically
- provide therapy
- carry the situation alone

---

## Strengths-Based Approach

It can be helpful to explore what supports the person.

Example prompts:

- “What has helped you get through difficult times before?”
- “What keeps you going right now?”
- “Who do you feel supported by?”

These questions can help identify:

- protective factors
- support systems
- sources of meaning

---

## Protective Factors

Things that can reduce risk include:

- strong social connections
- access to support
- coping skills
- hope or future thinking
- sense of purpose or value
- engagement with services

---

## Important Interaction Skills

When supporting someone:

- remain calm
- avoid judgement
- use simple, clear communication
- show empathy and genuine concern
- focus on listening over problem-solving

Additional principles:

- never agree to keep harmful situations secret
- consult others when unsure
- prioritise safety over certainty

---

## When to Escalate Immediately

Escalation is required if there are:

- thoughts of self-harm or suicide
- statements about wanting to disappear or die
- signs of extreme distress or loss of control
- credible concern for immediate safety

> If unsure → escalate. Safety > certainty.

---

## Final Reminder

> You are not expected to fix the situation - you are expected to respond safely.

Your presence, listening, and willingness to connect someone to help can make a meaningful difference.
