# Barriers to Help-Seeking

Many people who need support do not ask for it directly.

Distress is often:

- hidden
- minimised
- misunderstood

Your role is not to diagnose, but to:

- notice changes
- respond supportively
- escalate when needed

---

## Core Idea

> **People don’t avoid help because they are “fine” - they avoid help because of barriers.**

If barriers are reduced, help-seeking increases.

---

## Types of Barriers

Help-seeking barriers can be grouped into three categories:

- Internal (psychological)
- Social
- Structural

---

## 1. Internal Barriers

These occur within the individual.

### Common examples

- Shame or stigma

  > “Something is wrong with me”

- Minimisation

  > “It’s not that bad”

- Fear of consequences
  - academic impact
  - being judged

- Low self-worth

  > “I don’t deserve help”

- Difficulty recognising distress
  - especially in high-performing individuals

---

## 2. Social Barriers

These relate to how people perceive others and social environments.

### Common examples

- Fear of being seen as:
  - weak
  - incompetent
  - “not cut out for it”

- Comparison culture

  > “Everyone else is coping”

- Cultural factors
  - mental health not openly discussed

- Lack of trust in authority

  > “Staff won’t understand”

- Previous negative experiences

---

## 3. Structural Barriers

These are system-level challenges.

### Common examples

- Time pressure
  - deadlines > wellbeing

- Access issues
  - long wait times
  - difficulty accessing services

- Financial concerns
  - especially for some student populations

- Unclear pathways

  > “I don’t know where to go”

- Complex systems
  - referrals
  - paperwork
  - admin steps

---

## What This Means in Practice

Because of these barriers, people often:

- delay seeking help
- hide or mask distress
- only present when overwhelmed

> **The person who needs help most is often the least likely to ask for it.**

---

## Simple Observation Model

Instead of relying on someone asking for help, look for **changes**:

### Changes in:

- **Engagement**
  - engaged -> withdrawn

- **Behaviour**
  - cooperative -> resistant / shutdown

- **Communication**
  - clear -> short / disorganised

- **Effort**
  - consistent -> sudden drop

> **Behaviour is a signal, not a problem.**

---

## Common Early Warning Signs

- Sudden drop in performance or submissions
- Increased avoidance (absence, not responding)
- Unusual emotional responses (flat or intense)
- Increased frustration or agitation
- Appearing “fine” but disengaged

> “I’m fine” does not necessarily mean fine.

---

## When to Escalate

Escalation is required if there are signs of:

- self-harm or suicide
- wanting to disappear
- extreme distress or overwhelm
- significant behavioural change with impairment
- possible physical medical risk

> **If unsure -> escalate. Safety > certainty.**

---

## What Helps People Seek Help

Small actions can reduce barriers:

- Normalise struggle

  > “This happens to a lot of people”

- Be approachable and calm

- Ask simple, direct questions

  > “How are you going with everything?”

- Reduce friction
  - clearly point to services
  - make pathways obvious

- Follow up when appropriate

> **Small interactions reduce big barriers.**

---

## Final Reminder

> People do not need perfect support - they need accessible, human support.
